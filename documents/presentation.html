AI-Powered Credit Risk Intelligence Platform

An end-to-end credit decisioning system on the Home Credit Default Risk dataset: it scores an applicant's probability of default, explains the score in plain English, states the policy rules behind it, and answers questions about the portfolio in natural language.

The problem

A lender needs decisions that are fast, accurate, and defensible

In this dataset 8.073% of applicants default — roughly 1 in 12. Approving those loans costs written-off principal; refusing good applicants costs revenue. An underwriting desk therefore has six needs that a bare accuracy score does not meet.

1

Identify high-risk applicants early

Rank the book by risk before a human reads a file, so limited review capacity lands where losses actually are.

2

Automate the risk score itself

A calibrated probability per application, produced in one request, not a spreadsheet exercise per file.

3

Give a reason, not just a number

A declined applicant must be told which factors drove the decision, in language a non-analyst can act on.

4

Stand up to audit and regulation

Every figure traceable to a query or an artifact; the model's own limitations recorded rather than omitted.

5

Let non-technical staff query the data

A credit officer with a question about the portfolio should not need to file a ticket with an analyst.

6

Bridge the model into credit policy

A gradient-boosted ensemble cannot be written into a policy manual. A short list of measured rules can.

The business problem2

Scope

Six modules, one running application

1

Data pipeline

Kaggle download → DuckDB ingest of all 8 tables, with row counts verified against Kaggle's published figures on every build. 78.4s, 2.37 GB database.

2

Exploratory analysis

Six data-quality checks and eight business insights, each backed by its own SQL query and rendered chart — no hand-typed numbers.

3

Risk model

LightGBM over 205 features (122 raw + 83 engineered in SQL), isotonic-calibrated, with a four-way imbalance comparison behind the configuration.

4

Explainability

SHAP TreeExplainer per applicant, turned into a 2–4 sentence narrative grounded strictly on the computed contributions.

5

Business rules

A depth-4 surrogate tree distils the model into 16 IF-THEN rules, each with its measured population share and observed default rate.

6

Talk-to-data chatbot

Natural-language question → generated SQL → AST validation → read-only execution → an answer written only from the returned rows.

All six are served by one FastAPI process that also hosts the UI — one image, one port, identical behaviour locally and in Docker.

The data5

Data quality

Six checks, run against the live database

  • DAYS_EMPLOYED sentinel. 55,374 rows (18.0%) carry 365243 — 1,000 years — meaning "not employed", not a tenure. Left alone it would dominate any split on employment length. Mapped to NULL, with the fact kept as FLAG_NOT_EMPLOYED.
  • CODE_GENDER = 'XNA'. 4 rows. Negligible in volume, but an unhandled category would silently become its own one-hot column.
  • AMT_INCOME_TOTAL outlier. Maximum 117,000,000 against a median of 147,150 — a 795x ratio, one applicant, almost certainly a data-entry artefact. Handled by tree splits, which are scale-immune, rather than by deleting the row.
  • DAYS_* sign convention. All 4 non-DAYS_EMPLOYED date-offset columns checked for unexpected positive values. none found
  • Building-statistics missingness. The 42-column housing block is 48.8%–69.9% missing. Not random — it is absent for applicants whose housing type has no building record. Missingness is itself informative, so it is left as NaN for LightGBM rather than imputed.
  • Class imbalance. 8.07% positive, 11.4:1 — addressed with the documented four-way comparison on slide 10.

Business insight 2 of 3

Overdue debt elsewhere roughly doubles the default rate

16.2%default rate — any overdue balance on a bureau-reported credit
7.6%default rate — no overdue bureau credit
2.1xrelative risk, against a portfolio base rate of 8.07%

So what: bureau data surfaces problems that are invisible to the application form alone — obligations already in trouble at another lender. This is the clearest argument in the analysis for joining the child tables at all rather than modelling the application form on its own.

Business insight 3 of 3

Loan burden and default are not a straight line

8.9%default rate, middle quintile — average credit at 3.3x income
7.3%default rate, heaviest quintile — average credit at 8.2x income

So what: the most heavily indebted applicants are not the riskiest ones. A naive "flag high credit-to-income ratio" policy rule would miss the actual riskiest segment entirely.

This finding also caught a real bug: the insight function originally assumed the relationship was monotonic and read the riskiest quintile off the end of the sorted list. Both this and a reversed EXT_SOURCE lift calculation are now covered by regression tests with known, checkable answers.

Machine learning

Model choice, and the imbalance experiment

Why LightGBM. At ~307k rows and 205 mixed numeric/categorical features, gradient-boosted trees are the established strong baseline for tabular data at this scale — no GPU, minutes to train. LightGBM specifically for native NaN handling (the missingness here is informative, not random) and native categorical support, which avoids a 58-level one-hot expansion of ORGANIZATION_TYPE.

Model (identical feature set)Mean PR-AUC, 5-fold CV
Logistic Regression (transparent baseline)0.2518
LightGBM (shipped)0.2790

+10.8% relative over the linear reference — the honest answer to "how much does model choice buy," isolated from the feature-engineering contribution.

Four imbalance strategies, same CV splits, selected on PR-AUC:

StrategyPR-AUCROC-AUCBrier
None (shipped)0.27900.78590.0660
Random undersampling0.27250.78180.1884
SMOTE0.24920.76880.0675
scale_pos_weight (≈11.4)0.20000.73300.0733
None of the three standard imbalance fixes beat training on the data as-is. scale_pos_weight — the one most commonly reached for — is the worst performer here. Undersampling's Brier score of 0.1884, nearly 3x worse, shows why: discarding 90% of the majority class destroys the model's sense of how rare a default actually is, so its probabilities become badly overconfident.

The shipped model therefore uses no explicit imbalance handling. That is a measured result, not an assumption — and it runs against the common expectation that imbalance always needs correcting.

Explainability

Every score comes with its reasons — including the uncomfortable one

SHAP's TreeExplainer runs directly on the LightGBM booster. The factors and the score come from the same request, so an explanation can never disagree with the number it explains.

One distinction, stated because it is easy to get backwards: SHAP explains the model's raw log-odds output, not the calibrated probability shown to the user — calibration is a monotone remap fitted afterwards, with no per-feature decomposition. Contributions are therefore always presented directionally ("pushed the risk up / down"), never as an exact slice of the displayed percentage.

Top global features by mean |SHAP|:

1   EXT_SOURCE_MEAN0.404
2   CODE_GENDER0.104
3   ORGANIZATION_TYPE0.099
4   CREDIT_GOODS_RATIO0.093
CODE_GENDER is the model's #2 global feature — ahead of income, credit amount and employment history. Using a protected attribute as a predictor raises real fair-lending concerns. This is recorded as a limitation in the model card, not hidden: a production deployment would need to exclude protected attributes or apply an explicit fairness constraint and audit first.

Business rules

The model, distilled into a checklist a policy manual can hold

A depth-4 decision tree is fitted to approximate the calibrated score (not the label), then each leaf is reported as an IF-THEN rule. 16 rules were extracted, from 36.3% down to 2.6% observed default rate. Every rate below is measured directly against TARGET for the applicants actually in that leaf — not read off the tree's own prediction.

1

IF ext-source mean ≤ 0.253 AND ext-source min ≤ 0.121 AND credit-to-goods ratio > 1.16

→ 1.5% of applicants (4,554)  ·  observed default rate 36.3%  ·  4.5x base rate

3

IF ext-source mean ≤ 0.395 AND > 0.253 AND previous-refusal rate > 0.311 AND credit-to-goods ratio > 1.16

→ 1.3% of applicants (4,046)  ·  observed default rate 23.1%  ·  2.87x base rate

4

IF ext-source mean ≤ 0.253 AND ext-source min > 0.121 AND years employed ≤ 11.6

→ 2.1% of applicants (6,400)  ·  observed default rate 22.9%  ·  2.84x base rate

Fidelity, reported rather than assumed. The surrogate reaches R² = 0.4885 against the calibrated score and ROC-AUC 0.7207 against the actual label, versus the full model's 0.8766 on the same data. It captures real, useful structure but is meaningfully simpler than the model it approximates — a readable four-question checklist, not a substitute for the model in a decision that matters.

Talk-to-data

A plain-English question, an auditable answer

The pipeline

1. Schema card, pruned per questionTwo tiers — every table's purpose and join keys always; full column detail only for tables the question plausibly touches. Deterministic lexical selection, no embeddings.
2. Gemini writes SQLSees only columns that exist. Given an explicit way to refuse, so an unanswerable question need not be answered with an invented one.
3. sqlglot AST validationParsed, not regex-matched: one statement, read-only, no filesystem or network functions, every table and column resolved through CTEs and aliases, row limit injected.
4. Read-only DuckDB executionExternal access disabled, row- and time-bounded.
5. Gemini writes the answerFrom the returned rows only — the summarising model never sees the database.
Hallucination control is layered, and each layer catches a different failure. The prompt is not a security control. A rejected query gets exactly one repair attempt with the specific error — a second failure is reported as a failure, not retried until something passes. The SQL is always displayed, so an answer can be checked rather than trusted.

Honest assessment

Limitations, and what would come next

Known limitations

  • CODE_GENDER is the #2 feature by global mean |SHAP|, ahead of income, credit amount and employment history. A disparate-treatment and disparate-impact review would be required before any production use. This is a technical demonstration, not a compliance-reviewed scoring system.
  • The imbalance comparison used 4 well-known techniques at default settings. Cost-sensitive learning, ensemble methods such as BalancedRandomForest, and per-fold threshold tuning were not explored, and could change the "none wins" conclusion.
  • The 10:1 false-negative-to-false-positive cost ratio is asserted, not derived from real loan economics. It is a placeholder for whatever a deploying bank would supply — average loan size, cost of manual review, recovery rate on default.
  • Surrogate fidelity of R² 0.4885 is moderate. The 16 rules are a useful, auditable summary — not a substitute for the model in a decision that matters.
  • No temporal validation. The dataset carries no timestamp for out-of-time testing, so the random holdout split cannot speak to performance on genuinely future applications.

What would come next

  • Refit excluding protected attributes and measure the cost in PR-AUC, then decide explicitly whether that cost is acceptable — rather than leaving the current ranking in place by default.
  • Replace the asserted cost ratio with real loan economics and re-derive the operating threshold, which moves with it.
  • Extend the imbalance comparison to cost-sensitive and ensemble methods before treating "none wins" as settled.
  • Source a timestamped application feed to enable out-of-time validation and drift monitoring.
